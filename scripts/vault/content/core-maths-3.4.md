# Core Maths — Critical Analysis of Given Data and Models (3.4)

## C1 — Presenting logical and reasoned arguments

You are given a claim, conclusion or argument — often about data, a model or a financial decision — and must evaluate whether it actually follows. This is a written, reasoned response rather than a calculation.

Interrogate:

- Does the conclusion actually follow from the data given?
- Is the data relevant to the claim, or has something been substituted for it?
- Is the sample representative, or is there bias?
- Has a percentage change been misinterpreted (percentage vs percentage point)?
- Are figures given to a sensible degree of accuracy?
- Is the model based on assumptions that may not hold?

This is an **AO3** activity — devising strategies where the method is not obvious and communicating results. You are marked on making a realistic judgement and communicating it clearly in mathematical language, not on a single right answer.

## C2 — Communicating mathematical approaches and solutions

Summarising and report writing: take analysis you have done, or data you have been given, and present it as a written summary that is accurate, complete and suited to its audience.

Cover: the **context** (what is reported, over what period); only the **relevant figures**; correct mathematical **notation and units**; sensible **rounding** with the level of accuracy stated; **interpretation**, not transcription — say what the numbers mean; and the **limitations** — assumptions, bias, weak data.

Clarity and correct mathematical language earn the marks, not length. The spec assumes spreadsheets throughout: you are expected to read numerical data in tabular form, including formulae like `=SUM(A1:A10)`.

## C3 — Analysing critically

**Comparing a model with real data (C3.1).** Judge how well the model fits. Look for: a **systematic** difference (model values consistently above or below the real ones — a modelling problem, not random scatter); the **size** of the differences relative to the values involved; whether the discrepancy **matters** in context; and whether the model's assumptions hold for this data.

**Analysing data quoted in the media (C3.2).** Media, political and marketing data are misleading in recognisable ways:

| Trap | Example of the flaw |
| --- | --- |
| Truncated axis | A graph starting at y = 95 exaggerates a small change |
| Cherry-picked period | A trend shown over 3 months that reverses over 3 years |
| Percentage vs percentage point | "Unemployment rose 2%" when it went 3.9% → 5.9% — 2 points, a ~51% rise |
| Missing baseline | Comparing to a deliberately unrepresentative year |
| Survivorship / sampling bias | The data only covers those who responded |
| Correlation read as causation | Two things moving together is not one causing the other |

This topic draws on all of Paper 1: measures of centre and spread, percentages, and graph interpretation.
